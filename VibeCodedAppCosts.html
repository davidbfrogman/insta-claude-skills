A cost-cutting guide for vibe coders

Thirty ways to cut the bill on an app you built with AI.

Most vibe-coded apps don’t have a traffic problem. They have a defaults problem: the biggest model, the biggest server, a staging copy nobody opens, and a page that asks the database for news every second. This guide goes through all thirty fixes in the order I’d do them, with the price behind each one.

by Dave Brown · October 2026

When you ask an AI coding tool for “a backend,” it builds one that works. Working is all it was asked for. It reaches for the services it has seen most often and sizes them for a company that already has customers. Nothing about the output is wrong. It just costs more than an app with forty users needs to cost.

The good news is that the expensive parts follow a pattern, and it’s the same in nearly every app. Services you never touch. Environments that run all night. An AI model three sizes bigger than the job needs. Code that does the same work a thousand times when once would do. Each of those has a fix, and most of the fixes take an afternoon.

The thirty fixes are grouped into six parts. Each one says what to do, roughly how long it takes, what it saves, and what to watch out for. Prices came from web searches and vendor pages in October 2026. They change often, so check the vendor’s page before you commit to anything.

The rule everything else follows from

Cap the downside first, then follow the money. Set a spend cap today, before you optimise anything, because one bad month costs more than every saving in this guide put together. Then open the bill and start with the biggest line, not the easiest one.

For most vibe-coded apps the biggest line is the AI model. For the rest it’s usually a database or a server that’s far bigger than it needs to be. Either way, you’ll know within ten minutes of reading the invoice.

Part one · Where to start

Do the five-minute safety step first, then follow the money.

Thirty fixes is too many to do at once, and most of them won’t matter for your app. Here’s how to find the three or four that will.

Do fix 04, spend caps, before anything else, today, on every service that bills you. Then do fix 01: read the bill. Whatever the biggest line turns out to be tells you where to go next.

Part two · Right-size what you have

Most of what you’re paying for, you didn’t ask for.

Five fixes that change nothing about how the app works. You’re only getting rid of things that run without doing any useful work.

01

Read your bill line by line.

Right-size · Do this first

Open the billing page on every service you pay for and look at what’s actually there. AI coding tools build generously. Ask for “a backend” and you can end up with a database, a Redis cache, a staging copy of everything, a couple of storage buckets and a queue, and each of them bills you whether the app uses it or not.

For every line, you should be able to answer three questions: what is it, which part of the app uses it, and what breaks if I turn it off. If you can’t answer all three, that line is a candidate for deletion.

How to do it

Make a spreadsheet with one row per service, its cost last month and the month before, and the answers to those three questions. Sort by cost. The top three rows are almost always most of the bill, and that’s where your next hour should go.

Time

About an hour.

Saves

Whatever you find that you didn’t know was running.

Watch for

Deleting something that holds data. Export or snapshot it first.

02

Drop your server and database to the smallest tier that copes.

Right-size · Usually safe

Managed providers often suggest a production-sized instance by default, and AI tools tend to accept whatever is suggested. Most first apps spend nearly all day close to idle. Open the metrics for the last thirty days. If CPU rarely goes above about 20% and memory has room to spare, move down one size.

Wait a week and check again. If nothing got slower, go down another size. Doing it one step at a time means you find the floor without having to guess.

Time

Twenty minutes, then a week of watching.

Saves

Often half of that line or more, since each size down roughly halves the price.

Watch for

Memory, not CPU. That’s usually what runs out first on a small instance.

03

Put dev and staging to sleep when nobody’s working.

Right-size · Easy to automate

A week has 168 hours. If you work on the app 50 of them, a staging environment that’s only up during those hours costs about 30% of one that runs all the time. Every non-production copy of your app is a candidate: staging servers, dev databases, preview workers.

Most clouds let you stop instances on a schedule, and a two-line cron job will do it where they don’t. Better still, put non-production environments on platforms that scale to zero on their own (fix 08) or on preview deployments that disappear once the branch is merged. And ask honestly whether you need a staging environment yet. With no users there’s nothing to stage for, so you can test in production.

Time

An hour to automate.

Saves

About 70% of every non-production environment.

Watch for

Scheduled jobs that run in staging and that you forgot about.

04

Set billing alerts and hard spend caps on everything.

Right-size · Do this today

An alert tells you something is happening. A cap stops it. You want both, on every service that can bill you by usage. Set alerts at 50%, 90% and 100% of what you expect to spend, and send them to an inbox you actually read.

Providers differ on this. AWS and Google Cloud budgets alert you by default but don’t stop anything, so a real hard stop there takes extra setup. Others give you a real limit out of the box: Vercel’s spend management can pause a project, and Anthropic’s console lets you set a spend limit for each workspace. Wherever a real cap exists, turn it on.

Why this is first

The bills that hurt aren’t the slow creep. They’re a retry loop that runs all weekend, a function that calls itself, or an AI endpoint with no login that a bot finds on a Tuesday. You only find out when the invoice arrives, and by then the money is already spent. A cap turns a four-figure surprise into a short outage.

Time

Fifteen minutes per provider.

Saves

The one bad month.

Watch for

Providers where “budget” means an email, not a stop.

05

Delete old snapshots, backups, logs and orphaned volumes.

Right-size · Quiet pile-up

These are the lines that grow every month without anyone deciding they should. A disk that outlived the server it was attached to. Automated snapshots that are kept forever. Log groups that never expire, which is the default on AWS CloudWatch. Each one is small. Together they can add up to real money.

Set a retention policy and let it do the work. Keep 7 to 30 days of logs and a handful of recent backups, and delete any volume that isn’t attached to anything.

Time

Thirty minutes.

Saves

Small per item, and it grows every month you leave it.

Watch for

Keep at least one recent backup, and test that you can restore it.

Part three · Pick cheaper hosting models

Pay for running time only when someone’s actually using it.

An app with a few hundred users is idle most of the time. These seven fixes stop you paying for that idle time, and the last one keeps you from adding complexity that costs money to run.

06

Use free tiers aggressively, and read their terms.

Hosting · The whole line, until you outgrow it

Vercel, Netlify, Cloudflare, Supabase, Neon and Firebase all have free tiers big enough to run a real early-stage app. Vercel’s Hobby plan includes 100 GB of transfer and a million function invocations a month. (pricing) Supabase’s free tier gives you two projects and a 500 MB database. (pricing) Neon gives you 100 compute-hours and half a gigabyte of storage per project, on up to 100 projects. (pricing)

The trap

Read the terms as well as the limits. Vercel’s Hobby plan is for personal, non-commercial use, so once you charge money you’re meant to move to Pro at $20 per user per month. Supabase pauses free projects after a week with no activity. And check what happens when you go over a limit, because some services cut you off and others start billing you automatically.

Time

Varies. Cheapest when you choose at the start.

Saves

The entire line, until you cross the limit.

Watch for

Commercial-use clauses, pausing, and overage behaviour.

07

Host static frontends on a CDN, not a running server.

Hosting · Often the whole frontend bill

If your frontend builds to a folder of HTML, JavaScript and CSS (a Vite or Create React App project, or a static Astro site), it doesn’t need a server. It’s just files. Cloudflare Pages and Netlify serve those files from data centres close to your users, and you pay nothing or close to nothing, with no machine running in between.

AI tools often put the frontend in a Node container out of habit, next to the API. Split it out. The API can stay where it is.

Time

An afternoon.

Saves

The frontend server, usually all of it.

Watch for

Server-side rendering. If your framework renders pages on the server, the app isn’t fully static.

08

Use scale-to-zero platforms, so idle costs nothing.

Hosting · Best for spiky, low traffic

Google Cloud Run, AWS Lambda and Fly.io machines can shut down completely when no requests are coming in and start again on the next one. You pay for the seconds you actually serve traffic. For an API that gets a few thousand requests a day, that usually comes in under the price of the smallest always-on server, and Cloud Run and Lambda both include a monthly free allowance on top.

Time

A day, if the app is already in a container.

Saves

Every hour the app sits idle.

Watch for

Cold starts on the first request, and steady high traffic, where an always-on server becomes cheaper.

09

Consider one cheap VPS instead of many managed services.

Hosting · Five to ten times cheaper at small scale

A Hetzner server with 2 vCPUs and 4 GB of RAM costs single-digit euros a month (pricing), and DigitalOcean’s smallest droplets cost about the same. One of those can run your API, your background workers, a cache and a cron scheduler, all together. The same setup on managed services can easily come to five to ten times as much, mostly because each service charges its own minimum.

Tools like Coolify, Kamal or plain Docker Compose make deploying to one box feel close to a managed platform.

What you’re signing up for

You now own security updates, backups and uptime. None of that is hard, but all of it takes time. Keep the database on a managed service even if everything else moves to the VPS. That’s the one place where saving a few dollars isn’t worth the risk.

Time

A day to set up, plus a little ongoing upkeep.

Saves

The per-service minimums that add up across a managed stack.

Watch for

Unpatched servers and untested backups.

10

Consolidate. One Postgres can do the work of four services.

Hosting · Fewer bills, fewer limits

Early on, Postgres can be your queue (with a library like pg-boss), your cache, your full-text search, your document store (JSON columns), your pub/sub (LISTEN/NOTIFY) and your vector database (pgvector). Every separate service you drop is one less bill, one less free tier to outgrow and one less thing to keep running.

You’ll eventually outgrow some of this, and that’s fine. Moving the queue to a dedicated service when you have the traffic to justify it is easy. Paying for four services from day one, before there’s any traffic, is just paying.

Time

A few hours per service you fold in.

Saves

The minimum charge of each service you remove.

Watch for

Don’t migrate something that works and already costs nothing.

11

Use serverless Postgres that pauses when nobody’s querying it.

Hosting · Ideal for dev, staging and side projects

Neon shuts the database’s compute down after five minutes without queries and starts it again on the next one, so an idle database costs only its storage. (pricing) It also makes a cheap branch of your database for each preview environment.

Supabase works differently. Free projects pause after seven days without activity, and you have to restore them by hand, while paid Supabase compute runs around the clock. That makes Supabase a great free tier for prototypes, but it doesn’t scale to zero the way Neon does.

Time

An hour for a new project. A migration for an existing one.

Saves

Compute on every database that’s idle most of the day.

Watch for

A short wake-up delay on the first query after an idle period.

12

Avoid Kubernetes until you actually need it.

Hosting · A cost you’d add, not cut

On AWS, the EKS control plane alone costs $0.10 an hour, about $73 a month, before a single container runs. (pricing) Then come the minimum node counts, load balancers and monitoring, plus the hours you spend learning all of it. AI tools will cheerfully write Kubernetes manifests for an app with twelve users. Say no.

You need Kubernetes when you have many services, a team, and someone who wants to own the cluster. Until all three are true, a platform from fix 08 or one VPS from fix 09 will be cheaper and simpler.

Time

None. Just don’t.

Saves

$73/month in control-plane fees on EKS, plus nodes and the hours you’d spend.

Watch for

AI-generated infra code that adds it “to be production-ready.”

Part four · Cut AI and API costs

The line that grows with every user is the one to fix first.

For many vibe-coded apps this is the biggest line on the bill, because every click can trigger a model call, and the code that makes the call probably defaults to the biggest model with no limits.

The prices below are Anthropic’s current API rates per million tokens. (pricing) Other providers have the same structure: input is cheap, output costs about five times as much, and the gap between the smallest and largest models is large.

Model Input Output Cached input read
Claude Haiku 4.5$1$5$0.10
Claude Sonnet 5.5$2$10$0.20
Claude Opus 5.5$4$20$0.20
Claude Fable 5.1$10$50$0.25

Batch requests are half price on every model, cached reads included.

13

Use the smallest model that does the job.

AI costs · Usually the biggest single saving

Sorting a support ticket, pulling a date out of an email, tagging a photo, checking whether a comment is spam: these are small jobs, and Haiku handles them for a quarter of what Opus costs. Sonnet covers most writing, chat and coding. Save the largest models for the work that actually needs them.

Don’t guess, test. Take 20 to 50 real inputs from your app, run them through the smaller model, and read the outputs next to the ones you have now. If you can’t tell which is which, switch. On the newest models, also try turning the effort setting down before you change models. A capable model on low effort often beats a smaller model, and it uses fewer tokens than the same model on high.

Time

An afternoon of side-by-side testing.

Saves

Up to 75% per call, going from Opus 5.5 to Haiku 4.5.

Watch for

A quality drop you only notice weeks later. Keep the test set and re-run it.

14

Cache AI responses for prompts you’ve already answered.

AI costs · Free after the first call

If ten users ask your app to summarise the same article, you only need to pay for the first summary. Store each response in your database, keyed by a hash of the model, the prompt and the inputs, and check there before you call the model. Product descriptions, FAQ answers and summaries of shared documents all repeat more than you’d expect.

Caching prompts that are similar rather than identical (by comparing embeddings) saves more, but it can also give a user the wrong answer. Start with exact matches. They’re safe, and they’re often a bigger share of traffic than people assume.

Time

A couple of hours.

Saves

Every repeated call, in full.

Watch for

Caching anything personal across users. Include the user in the key when it matters.

15

Turn on prompt caching for long, repeated system prompts.

AI costs · Pays off from the second request

If every call sends the same long system prompt, the same tool definitions or the same reference document, prompt caching lets the provider reuse that part instead of charging full price every time. On most Claude models, cached reads cost a tenth of the normal input price. On Opus 5.5 they cost a twentieth. Writing to the cache costs 1.25 times normal for a five-minute cache, so it pays for itself from the second request.

Two things that quietly break it

The cached part has to be byte-for-byte identical every time, so a timestamp or a user’s name near the top of the system prompt ruins it. Put everything that changes at the end. And there’s a minimum size: 512 tokens on the newest models, but 4,096 on Haiku 4.5, so a short prompt won’t cache there at all. Check cache_read_input_tokens in the response. If it’s always zero, caching isn’t working.

Time

An hour, mostly reordering the prompt.

Saves

Up to 90% (95% on Opus 5.5) of the repeated part of every input.

Watch for

Dynamic content at the top of the prompt.

16

Trim your prompts and cap the output length.

AI costs · You pay per token both ways

AI-written prompts tend to grow. Instructions pile up, examples get added and never removed, and whole documents get pasted in when a single section would do. Read your system prompt the way a stranger would and cut anything that doesn’t change the output.

Output is where the money goes, at five times the input price. If your interface shows two sentences, ask for two sentences and set max_tokens to match. The instruction is what keeps answers short. The cap is a backstop for when the model ignores it. Don’t set the cap so low that answers get cut off halfway, because a truncated answer you have to retry costs more than a complete one.

Time

An hour per prompt.

Saves

Often a third to a half of output spend.

Watch for

Truncated answers. Check how often responses hit the cap.

17

Send work nobody is waiting on through a batch API.

AI costs · Half price, no quality trade-off

Anthropic’s Message Batches API and OpenAI’s Batch API both charge half price in exchange for results arriving within 24 hours, which in practice is usually much sooner. The discount stacks with prompt caching. Nightly digests, tagging a backlog, re-summarising old records and running evals are all work nobody watches happen, so there’s no reason to pay full price for it.

Time

Half a day per job you move.

Saves

50% of everything you move.

Watch for

Results arrive in any order. Match them up by ID, not by position.

18

Rate-limit every user, so one person can’t run up your bill.

AI costs · Protection, not optimisation

Without a limit, one user with a script, or one user who simply loves your app, can make thousands of model calls a day at your expense. Put a per-user limit on every route that calls a model, per minute and per day. Give free users a smaller allowance than paying ones, and record spend per user so you can see who’s costing what.

The endpoint to check tonight

Any API route that calls a model without checking who’s logged in is a free, public proxy to your credit card. AI-generated code ships these all the time, usually as a “quick test” route that never got removed. Search for every model call and confirm there’s an auth check in front of it.

Time

Two hours.

Saves

The worst case, which is unbounded.

Watch for

Limits keyed on IP address only. They’re easy to get around.

19

Cache third-party API results instead of calling every time.

AI costs · Same idea, other APIs

Maps, geocoding, weather, company lookups and currency rates usually return the same answer to the same question for minutes, hours or forever. Store the result with an expiry that suits the data: fifteen minutes for weather, a day for exchange rates, and much longer for an address you’ve already geocoded.

Time

An hour per API.

Saves

Most of those calls, on anything people look up repeatedly.

Watch for

Terms of service. Some providers, Google Maps among them, limit how long you may store results.

What it adds up to

Here’s one AI feature with each fix applied in turn: a chat assistant with a 3,000-token system prompt, about 400 tokens of user input and 600 tokens of output, called 5,000 times a day, or 150,000 times a month.

Change Per month Running saving
As generated: Opus 5.5, no caching, no cap$3,840–
Fix 13: switch to Sonnet 5.5$1,92050%

Assumes nearly every request lands within five minutes of another, so the system prompt is almost always read from cache, and ignores the small cost of writing to the cache. Your numbers will differ. The order in which the savings show up usually won’t.

Why the cheapest model isn’t automatically the answer

Haiku 4.5 on the same feature, with the same output cap, comes to about $735 a month. That’s more than Sonnet 5.5 with caching, because this 3,000-token prompt is under Haiku’s 4,096-token caching minimum, so Haiku pays full price for it on every call. The fixes interact. Run the numbers on your own prompt before you assume smaller means cheaper.

To find all of this in your own code, paste the prompt below into Claude Code, Cursor, or whatever tool built the app. It only reports. It won’t change anything until you tell it to.

Copy this 

Find every place this app calls an AI model or a paid third-party API.

For each one, give me a table with:
- file and line
- provider and model
- what the call is for
- roughly how many input tokens it sends, including the system prompt
- the max output tokens setting, or "none"
- whether prompt caching is turned on
- whether the response is stored and reused anywhere
- whether there is a login check and a per-user rate limit in front of it

Then, without changing any code, recommend:
1. Calls that could use a smaller model because the task is simple
   (classification, extraction, tagging, short rewrites, yes/no checks).
2. Calls that send the same long prompt every time and should use
   prompt caching. Flag anything dynamic near the top of the prompt.
3. Calls with no output cap, and a sensible cap for each.
4. Calls nobody waits on that could go through a batch API.
5. Calls repeated with the same input that should be cached.
6. Any route that calls a model with no login check. List these first.

Rank everything by how much money it is likely to save, biggest first.
Ask me before you change anything.

Part five · Reduce bandwidth and storage

Every byte you serve twice is a byte you pay for twice.

Bandwidth is the cost people don’t see coming, because it grows with success. These five fixes are cheap to do now and expensive to put off.

20

Put a CDN in front of everything.

Bandwidth · Cloudflare’s free plan is enough

A CDN keeps copies of your files at the edge, so repeat requests never reach your server or your storage bucket and never count against their egress. Cloudflare’s free plan handles this for most small apps. Point your domain at it and you’re most of the way there.

The rest is cache headers. Give static assets with hashed filenames a long Cache-Control lifetime, so the CDN and the browser both keep them, and every visit after the first costs you nothing.

Time

An hour.

Saves

Most egress on static files.

Watch for

Caching pages that contain one user’s data. Cache files, not personal responses.

21

Compress and resize images, and serve WebP or AVIF.

Bandwidth · Usually the heaviest thing on the page

A phone photo is 3 to 5 MB. If you display it 400 pixels wide, nearly all of that download is wasted. Resize images when they’re uploaded, keep two or three sizes, and serve WebP or AVIF with a srcset so each device downloads the size it needs. Modern formats are usually a quarter to a half smaller than JPEG at the same quality.

Time

An afternoon.

Saves

Often most of the bytes on image-heavy pages.

Watch for

Paid image services that bill per transformation. Resize once at upload, not on every request.

22

Move files to object storage with no egress fees.

Bandwidth · The fee that grows with success

On most storage the expensive part isn’t keeping the file. It’s serving it. Cloudflare R2 charges $0.015 per GB per month for storage and nothing for egress, at any volume, with 10 GB free. (pricing) Backblaze B2 is cheap to store on and includes a generous free egress allowance. Both work with the S3 API, so for most apps moving over means changing the endpoint and the keys.

Time

Half a day, plus copying existing files.

Saves

All egress on stored files.

Watch for

Operation charges. R2 bills writes at $4.50 per million, which matters if you write many small files.

23

Set lifecycle rules to move or delete old files automatically.

Storage · Set once, saves forever

Temporary uploads, generated exports, old versions and abandoned drafts pile up in storage forever unless something deletes them. A lifecycle rule handles it: delete temp files after a day, delete exports after a week, and move anything older than 90 days to a cheaper tier. R2’s Infrequent Access tier costs $0.01 per GB per month instead of $0.015.

Time

Thirty minutes.

Saves

Storage that would otherwise only grow.

Watch for

Infrequent-access tiers charge to read files back and have minimum storage durations.

24

Turn on gzip or Brotli compression for every response.

Bandwidth · Five minutes to check

HTML, JavaScript, CSS and JSON usually compress by two-thirds or more. Most CDNs and hosting platforms compress automatically, but API responses from your own server often aren’t compressed, and a JSON list endpoint is exactly where it adds up. Check with one command:

Run this 

curl -sI -H 'Accept-Encoding: br, gzip' https://yourapp.com/api/items | grep -i content-encoding

If that prints nothing, the response isn’t compressed.

Time

Five minutes to check, an hour to fix.

Saves

Two-thirds or more of text bandwidth.

Watch for

Compressing twice, at the app and at the CDN. Pick one.

Part six · Fix the code AI wrote

AI writes code that works. It doesn’t write code that’s cheap to run.

These five patterns are fine with ten users and expensive with ten thousand. The prompt at the end of this section will find them in your codebase for you.

25

Find the N+1 queries and the missing indexes.

Code · The most common database cost

An N+1 query is a list page that loads 50 orders, then runs one more query per order to fetch its customer. That’s 51 trips to the database where one join would do. AI-generated code is full of them, because they’re the most direct way to write it, and they’re invisible until the list gets long.

Missing indexes are the other half. A query that filters on user_id with no index on that column reads the whole table. With a thousand rows nobody notices. With a million, the database gets slow and you end up paying for a bigger one. Most managed Postgres dashboards list your slowest queries, so start there, and run EXPLAIN on each one to see whether it’s scanning the whole table.

Time

An hour per slow page.

Saves

The database upgrade you were about to buy.

Watch for

Indexing everything. Each index slows writes and takes up storage.

26

Stop polling. Use webhooks or realtime subscriptions.

Code · Costs money even while users are idle

A page that checks for updates every second makes 86,400 requests a day for each open tab. A hundred users who leave the tab open overnight add up to 8.6 million requests, and nearly every answer is “nothing new.” On a platform that bills per invocation, you pay for every one.

Use a webhook when an outside service is the one with news (Stripe, for example, can tell you when a payment clears). Use a realtime subscription, through Supabase Realtime, Firebase listeners or server-sent events, when your own data changes. If you really have to poll, poll slowly, back off when nothing changes, and stop when the tab is hidden.

Time

Half a day per polling loop.

Saves

Almost all of those requests.

Watch for

Polling hidden inside a useEffect with a setInterval.

27

Paginate queries instead of loading entire tables.

Code · Cheap now, essential later

SELECT * FROM posts to render the first twenty posts works on day one. Later it loads every row and every column, sends all of it over the network, and keeps it in memory, just to show a screenful. Add a LIMIT, use cursor-based pagination for anything that scrolls, and select only the columns the page actually uses.

Time

An hour per list.

Saves

Database load, memory and bandwidth, in proportion to table size.

Watch for

Large OFFSET values, which get slow. Cursors scale better.

28

Turn down the logging.

Code · Billed by the gigabyte

Logging and monitoring services usually charge by the volume they take in. AI-generated code logs freely: inside loops, on every request, and sometimes whole request bodies, which is a privacy problem as well as a cost. Set production to warn or info, remove debug logs from hot paths, sample high-volume events instead of recording every one, and set a retention period.

Time

An hour.

Saves

Often most of the logging bill.

Watch for

Removing the logs you’ll need at 2 a.m. Keep errors and slow requests.

29

Kill runaway cron jobs and background workers.

Code · The schedule nobody revisited

A job that runs every minute when hourly would do costs sixty times as much. A worker that loops forever, checking a queue that’s usually empty, keeps a machine awake that could otherwise scale to zero. List every scheduled job and every always-on worker, and for each one ask what would break if it ran a tenth as often. Usually the answer is nothing.

Time

Thirty minutes to list them.

Saves

Invocations, and the idle machines those jobs keep awake.

Watch for

The same job scheduled in two places, in code and in the platform’s dashboard.

Fixes 25 to 29 are easy to find with the same tool that wrote the code. This prompt asks for a report only, so nothing changes until you choose what to fix.

Copy this 

Audit this codebase for patterns that make hosting cost more than it
needs to. Look for:

1. N+1 queries: a database query inside a loop, or a list page that
   runs one extra query per row.
2. Missing indexes: columns used in WHERE, JOIN or ORDER BY on tables
   that will grow, with no index on them.
3. Polling: setInterval, useEffect or cron loops that check for changes
   every few seconds.
4. Unbounded queries: SELECTs with no LIMIT or pagination, or code that
   loads a whole table into memory.
5. Noisy logging: logs inside loops or on every request at debug/info
   level in production, and anything that logs full request bodies.
6. Scheduled jobs and background workers that run more often than the
   data actually changes.

For each finding give me: the file and line, what it does, what it
costs (more database load, more function invocations, more log volume),
and the fix. Group findings by severity, worst first.

Part seven · Get free money

Someone will pay your bill for a year if you ask.

One fix, and it’s the only one in this guide that takes nothing but an application form.

30

Apply for startup credits.

Free money · Apply to all of them

Every major cloud and several AI providers give credits to startups, because a startup that builds on their platform tends to stay there. The amounts depend on whether you have funding and who refers you, but even the self-serve tiers are worth the twenty minutes the form takes.

Program What’s on offer Who qualifies
AWS Activate $1,000 self-serve. Up to $100,000 through a partner VC, accelerator or incubator. Any founder for the self-serve tier.
Google for Startups Up to $200,000 over two years, and up to $350,000 for AI-first startups. Larger tiers need funding. Smaller ones exist for earlier companies.

Credits expire, and they hide the real bill

Most credits run out after one or two years. Build as if you’re paying full price, and keep the spend caps from fix 04 in place, so that when the credits end it isn’t a surprise. And don’t choose a cloud for its credits alone. Moving off it later can cost more than the credits were worth.

Time

Twenty minutes per application.

Saves

Months to years of the bill.

Watch for

Expiry dates, and credits that only cover some services.

Read down the time column and most entries are an hour or less. Read down the last column and most of them say “when the bill tells you to.” That’s the point. You don’t need to do thirty things. You need to read one invoice, find the line that’s out of proportion, and fix that one first. Then do it again next month.

It’s also worth saying that a small bill is fine. Paying $25 a month for a database you never have to think about is a good deal. The aim isn’t zero. The aim is that every dollar on the bill pays for something a user actually touches, and that a bug can’t spend money you don’t have.

Your app doesn’t have to be cheap. It has to cost money only when someone is using it.
